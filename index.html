What is Khaana?

खाना, khaana, food

Khaana (खाना in Devanagari) is a familiar Hindi and Urdu word tied to food, meals, and eating. Khaana.org explores the word, what it actually means, where it comes from, and how the idea of "food" is expressed across South Asia's many languages.

Spelling & pronunciation

Spell it "khaana," with a double A

Khaana.org's recommended English spelling is khaana, with two a's. The two a's aren't there for looks. Each one stands for a separate vowel sound, and both are held long.

खाना khaana

Each of those two "a"s is a separate vowel sound, held long (like the "a" in "father," not the short "u" in "but"). Spell the word with a single a, as in "khana," and you drop one of those sounds. It's a common shortcut, but it points to the wrong pronunciation. Writing khaana keeps both sounds, which is why this site uses and recommends it.

Devanagari

In Hindi's native Devanagari script, the word is written खाना. Urdu, which uses the Perso-Arabic script instead, writes the same word as کھانا. Same word, same pronunciation, different writing system, because Hindi and Urdu share most of their everyday vocabulary while writing it differently.

Formal transliteration: khānā

Linguists and dictionaries typically transliterate खाना as khānā, using a macron (the line over the a) to mark a long vowel. This is part of IAST (International Alphabet of Sanskrit Transliteration) and similar systems used in academic writing on Hindi and Urdu.

Common spelling: khaana

Outside of academic writing, most people don't have easy access to a macron, so the long ā gets written by simply doubling the letter: khaana. This is a widely understood convention in informal Romanized Hindi (sometimes called "Hinglish"): a doubled vowel signals "hold this sound longer" the same way the macron does.

Why the doubled "aa"?

Hindi distinguishes between a short a (अ, as in the "u" in "but") and a long ā (आ, held roughly twice as long, closer to the "a" in "father"). Both syllables in खाना carry that long ā, which is exactly what the four-piece breakdown above shows: kha already includes it, and so does na. Writing khana with a single a drops that length from both. Writing khaana keeps it, which is why this site treats it as the better everyday spelling.

How to say it

Roughly: KHAA-naa, two syllables, stress on the first, both vowels held long. The "kh" is a breathy, aspirated "k" (a puff of air after the consonant, similar to the "k" in "Khan"), not the same as a plain English "k."

Meaning

Does khaana mean "food," or "to eat"?

Both, depending on how it's used. Khaana is a noun meaning "food" or "a meal," and it's also the infinitive form of the verb "to eat." Hindi grammar is why the same word does both jobs.

As a noun: food, a meal

Used as a noun, खाना (khaana) simply means "food" or "a meal," in the general sense, not any specific dish.

Noun
खाना तैयार है।
Khaana taiyār hai.
The food is ready.
Noun
मुझे घर का खाना पसंद है।
Mujhe ghar kā khaana pasand hai.
I like home-cooked food.

As a verb: to eat

खाना (khaana) is also the infinitive form of the Hindi verb "to eat." Hindi infinitives regularly end in -nā (करना/karnā "to do," जाना/jānā "to go," खाना/khaana "to eat"), so this is not a coincidence of two look-alike words. It is one word, behaving the way Hindi infinitives behave.

Verb
मैं खाना खा रहा हूँ।
Maiṅ khaana khā rahā hūṅ.
I am eating (food).
Verb
क्या तुमने खाना खाया?
Kyā tumne khaana khāyā?
Did you eat?
Why one word covers both: the sentence "मैं खाना खा रहा हूँ" (Maiṅ khaana khā rahā hūṅ) literally uses खाना twice, once as the noun object ("food") and once inside the conjugated verb ("am eating"). That is not repetition for effect. It is simply how the grammar works, and it shows the noun and verb senses sitting side by side in ordinary speech.

Context does the work

In practice, Hindi and Urdu speakers rarely find this ambiguous. "खाना बनाना" (khaana banānā) means "to cook" (literally, "to make food"). "खाना खाना" (khaana khaana) means "to eat" (literally, "to eat food," using both senses back to back). Sentence position and the surrounding grammar make the intended meaning clear, the same way "watch" can be a noun or a verb in English without causing confusion.

History & etymology

Where does khaana come from?

Its roots trace back through Hindi and Urdu to a common Indo-Aryan verb for eating, with a false-friend mix-up along the way worth knowing about.

A shared Indo-Aryan root

Hindi and Urdu both descend from Sanskrit and its later spoken forms (Prakrit, then Apabhramsha), and खाना/کھانا (khaana) is generally traced to the Sanskrit verbal root khād- ("to chew, to eat"), seen in the Sanskrit verb खादति (khādati, "eats"). Over centuries of sound change as spoken Sanskrit evolved into the Prakrits and eventually into Hindi-Urdu, that root produced खाना (khaana), the modern infinitive "to eat," which (as covered in the meaning section) also became the everyday noun for "food."

One word, two scripts

Hindi and Urdu are, at the everyday spoken level, extremely close, often treated by linguists as two registers of a single Hindustani language, diverging mainly in their formal/literary vocabulary and their writing systems (Devanagari for Hindi, Perso-Arabic script for Urdu). खाना (Hindi) and کھانا (Urdu) are the same word, inherited from the same source, simply written differently. It's a useful reminder that script and language aren't the same thing.

The Persian false friend: -khana

Here is where it gets confusing, and where a lot of casual internet explanations go wrong. There's a completely different word, Persian خانه (khāna, "house" or "room"), that also gets Romanized as "khana." It shows up as a suffix in many Hindi/Urdu compound words borrowed from Persian: kārkhānā ("workhouse," i.e. factory), dawākhānā ("medicine-house," i.e. pharmacy or clinic), ḍākkhānā ("mail-house," i.e. post office).

This Persian khāna ("house") and the Hindi-Urdu khaana ("food"/"to eat") covered on this site are unrelated words from different language families that happen to sound and Romanize almost identically. Sanskrit and Persian belong to different branches of the broader Indo-European language family, and this pair is a coincidence of sound, not a shared origin. In careful transliteration, the vowel length and stress often differ slightly between the two, but in everyday Roman spelling, the overlap is a genuine, common source of confusion.

Across languages

Does every South Asian language say "khaana"?

Not necessarily, and that's the interesting part. South Asia has dozens of languages from at least two entirely unrelated language families. Some share khaana's root, some share only its verb, and some are completely unrelated.

Two language families, one region

Most North and West Indian languages, along with Urdu, Nepali, and Bengali, belong to the Indo-Aryan branch of the Indo-European language family, the same broad family English belongs to, however distantly. Most South Indian languages, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, and Malayalam, belong to a completely separate family, Dravidian, with no shared ancestry with Sanskrit or Hindi at all. That split is the main reason "food" vocabulary looks so different from one end of India to the other.

What "related" means

A few rows are worth a closer look:

Punjabi and Nepali are close enough to Hindi, and geographically and historically intertwined enough, that they use essentially the same word. Marathi, Gujarati, and Bengali are also Indo-Aryan and do preserve a form of the same kha- verb root for "to eat," but their everyday noun for "food" drifted to a different derived word over time. That is a normal thing for related languages to do. They keep a shared root and build different words on top of it.

Telugu, Tamil, Kannada, and Malayalam aren't Indo-Aryan at all, so there's no shared root to find. Their food words (often borrowed from Sanskrit for more formal terms, like āhāram) come from an entirely separate linguistic history. Any resemblance to Hindi vocabulary in Dravidian languages tends to come from separate, later Sanskrit borrowing, not a common ancestor.

This table is a starting reference, not an exhaustive survey of every regional dialect or register. Food vocabulary varies further by region, formality, and community even within a single language. Corrections and additions from speakers of these languages are welcome.
