History & etymology

Where does khaana come from?

Its roots trace back through Hindi and Urdu to a common Indo-Aryan verb for eating, with a false-friend mix-up along the way worth knowing about.

A note on sourcing: etymology is a specialist field, and word histories are easy to get subtly wrong from general reading alone. This page summarizes what standard comparative Indo-Aryan references (such as R. L. Turner's A Comparative Dictionary of the Indo-Aryan Languages) commonly say about this word family. It has not yet been reviewed by a Hindi-Urdu linguist. If you have expertise here and see something imprecise, please get in touch, corrections are genuinely welcome.

A shared Indo-Aryan root

Hindi and Urdu both descend from Sanskrit and its later spoken forms (Prakrit, then Apabhramsha), and खाना/کھانا (khānā) is generally traced to the Sanskrit verbal root khād- ("to chew, to eat"), seen in the Sanskrit verb खादति (khādati, "eats"). Over centuries of sound change as spoken Sanskrit evolved into the Prakrits and eventually into Hindi-Urdu, that root produced खाना (khānā), the modern infinitive "to eat," which (as covered on the meaning page) also became the everyday noun for "food."

One word, two scripts

Hindi and Urdu are, at the everyday spoken level, extremely close, often treated by linguists as two registers of a single Hindustani language, diverging mainly in their formal/literary vocabulary and their writing systems (Devanagari for Hindi, Perso-Arabic script for Urdu). खाना (Hindi) and کھانا (Urdu) are the same word, inherited from the same source, simply written differently. It's a useful reminder that script and language aren't the same thing.

The Persian false friend: -khana

Here's where it gets genuinely confusing, and where a lot of casual internet explanations go wrong. There's a completely different word, Persian خانه (khāna, "house" or "room"), that also gets Romanized as "khana." It shows up as a suffix in many Hindi/Urdu compound words borrowed from Persian: kārkhānā ("workhouse," i.e. factory), dawākhānā ("medicine-house," i.e. pharmacy or clinic), ḍākkhānā ("mail-house," i.e. post office).

This Persian khāna ("house") and the Hindi-Urdu khānā ("food"/"to eat") covered on this site are unrelated words from different language families that happen to sound and Romanize almost identically. Sanskrit and Persian belong to different branches of the broader Indo-European language family, and this pair is a coincidence of sound, not a shared origin. In careful transliteration, the vowel length and stress often differ slightly between the two, but in everyday Roman spelling, the overlap is a genuine, common source of confusion.

What we're not claiming

We're deliberately not presenting a fully footnoted academic etymology here. Historical linguistics involves real debate over reconstructed forms and sound changes, and getting it right takes training we don't want to pretend to have. Consider this page a reliable starting orientation, not a citation-ready source, until it's been reviewed by someone with the right expertise.
